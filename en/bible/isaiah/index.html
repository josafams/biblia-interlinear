Isaiah יְשַׁעְיָהוּ

Isaiah gathers oracles linked to the prophet Isaiah son of Amoz, who was active in Jerusalem in the second half of the 8th century BC, when Assyria dominated the region. Chapters 1 to 39 denounce Judah's social injustice and empty worship, warn the kings against alliances with foreign powers, announce judgment on the neighboring nations and end with accounts of King Hezekiah and Sennacherib's invasion. From chapter 40 on, the setting is the exile in Babylon and the return: God comforts Jerusalem, calls Cyrus, king of Persia, His anointed and presents a “servant” whose suffering brings restoration to others. The final chapters speak of a rebuilt community, open to foreigners, and end with the vision of new heavens and a new earth. Throughout the book, God is often called “the Holy One of Israel.”

Structure

  1. Judah and Jerusalem before God chs. 1–12

    Denunciations and promises about Jerusalem, the vision of the call (ch. 6) and the oracles of the time of King Ahaz, with the signs of Immanuel and of the shoot of Jesse.

  2. Oracles about the nations chs. 13–23

    Messages about Babylon, Assyria, Philistia, Moab, Damascus, Cush, Egypt, Edom, Arabia, Tyre and Jerusalem itself.

  3. Judgment and feast for the whole earth chs. 24–27

    Poems of universal scope: the devastated earth, the banquet prepared on the mountain and the defeat of Leviathan.

  4. Woes and promises chs. 28–35

    Criticism of the leaders of Ephraim and Judah and of seeking help from Egypt, followed by promises of a righteous king and of the desert that blooms.

  5. Narratives about Hezekiah chs. 36–39

    The Assyrian siege of Jerusalem, the king's illness and healing and the visit of the envoys from Babylon; the passage closely parallels 2 Kings 18–20.

  6. Comfort and deliverance chs. 40–55

    Announcement of the end of the exile, polemic against idols, the mission of Cyrus and the poems about the servant of the LORD.

  7. Zion restored chs. 56–66

    Exhortations to a community that has returned to the land, criticism of worship and injustice, the future glory of Jerusalem and the promise of new heavens and a new earth.
